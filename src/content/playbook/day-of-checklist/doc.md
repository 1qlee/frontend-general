## The short version

- [ ] **Night before:** confirm logistics, test your setup, rehearse your opener and two stories out loud, prepare questions, stop studying, sleep.
- [ ] **Morning:** 20-minute warm-up (below), no new material.
- [ ] **10 minutes before:** water, bathroom, notifications off, tabs closed, blank notes doc open, three slow breaths.
- [ ] **In the room:** structure out loud, think out loud, ask clarifying questions, check in with the interviewer.
- [ ] **After:** debrief within an hour, thank-you note within 24 hours.

> [!TIP]
> You've done the work. Today is about **showing** what you know, not learning more. Every item below either removes a source of friction or puts you in the right frame of mind.

## The night before

### Logistics

- [ ] Every round's **time in your time zone**, its **type** (coding, UI build, system design, behavioural, hiring manager) and the **interviewer's name and role**
- [ ] Links for video calls and coding pads open correctly; the recruiter's phone number is saved in case a link fails
- [ ] A quick look at each interviewer's public profile: their team, stack, and anything you might genuinely ask about
- [ ] Which playbook doc to skim for each round:

| Round | Skim in the morning |
|---|---|
| Coding | The coding round (six-step script, phrase bank) + JS & TS cheat sheet |
| UI build | The UI build round (45-minute plan, prompt gotchas) + React cheat sheet |
| System design | Frontend system design (RADIO table, clarifying questions) + web platform numbers |
| Behavioural / hiring manager | Behavioural (STAR, story bank, 15 questions) |
| AI-assisted / "how do you work?" | AI-assisted development (positioning sentence, workflow loop) |

### Material

- [ ] Say "**tell me about yourself**" out loud twice, timed (60–90 s)
- [ ] Rehearse **two** stories from your story bank out loud: your proudest project and a conflict or failure
- [ ] Write **"why this company"** notes: one product detail, one engineering detail (blog post, open-source work, talk), one reason it fits what you want next
- [ ] Write **2–3 questions per interviewer** (see the lists below)
- [ ] Put the opening scripts on a sticky note or a second screen: the coding six steps, the UI plan, RADIO

### Body

- [ ] **Stop studying** in the evening. Light flashcard review is fine; a new hard topic at 11 p.m. only adds anxiety.
- [ ] Clothes, water bottle, charger and notepad ready
- [ ] Two alarms; aim for a full night's sleep
- [ ] Plan the morning with buffer: eat, move, and avoid back-to-back meetings before the first round

## Remote setup checks

Do these **the night before** and again **15 minutes before** the first round.

## The 20-minute warm-up in this app

A warm-up is about **getting your voice and brain into explaining mode**, not about learning. Speak every answer out loud.

| Minutes | Activity | Where |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Flashcards: **Due now** (or **By day** for today's topics). Say each answer out loud before revealing | Flashcards |
| 5–10 | **Drill weak spots** (Dashboard), or **Rapid-fire concepts** if you have none. Answer 2–3 questions out loud: one-sentence answer first, then detail, then a trade-off | Dashboard / Mock interview |
| 10–15 | The round-specific drill (table below) | Practice bank / Mock interview / Playbook |
| 15–20 | Skim the matching playbook doc and read your mindset scripts. Then **stop** | Playbook |

| Today's round | Minutes 10–15 drill |
|---|---|
| Coding | Re-solve one exercise you've **already** solved (e.g. `debounce` or `Promise.all`) from the Practice bank, narrating the six steps out loud. Not a new hard problem |
| UI build | Read the 45-minute plan and the gotchas for two likely prompts; type the `AsyncState` union and `useDebouncedValue` from memory |
| System design | Say the RADIO opener and five clarifying questions out loud; skim the rendering and real-time tables |
| Behavioural | "Tell me about yourself" twice with a timer; skim the story-bank table and the 15 questions |
| AI-assisted | Say the one-sentence positioning and the workflow loop; answer one question in the **AI-assisted development** mock |

> [!MISTAKE]
> Doing a brand-new hard problem the morning of the interview. If it goes badly, you walk in rattled. Re-solve something you know: the goal is fluency and confidence.

## Mindset scripts

### Before you join

- **Name the feeling and reframe it:** "This is my body getting ready to perform." Nervous energy and excitement feel physically the same; telling yourself "I'm excited" tends to work better than "calm down".
- **Breathe:** box breathing: in for 4, hold for 4, out for 4, hold for 4, four times. Or two short inhales through the nose and a long exhale.
- **Reframe the interviewer:**

> [!INTERVIEW] Say this to yourself
> "The interviewer wants me to pass. A good hire ends their search. My job isn't to be perfect; it's to show them how I think, clearly, so they can write down evidence."

### When you blank

1. **Pause on purpose:** "Let me take a moment to think about that." Silence of 10–20 seconds feels long to you and normal to them.
2. **Restate the question** in your own words. It buys time and often unlocks the answer.
3. **Write down what you *do* know**: inputs, outputs, an example, a structure (RADIO, STAR, the six steps).
4. **Start from the structure, not the answer:** "I'll start with the requirements…"

### When you don't know something

> [!INTERVIEW] Honest and still strong
> "I haven't used that directly, so I don't want to guess at the API. Here's how I'd reason about it from what I do know: …, and here's how I'd verify it."

Bluffing is far more damaging than a clear "I don't know, but here's my reasoning".

### When a round goes badly

- Rounds are usually **scored independently**, often by different people. One weak round rarely sinks a loop on its own.
- Many candidates who receive offers were sure they'd bombed a round. You are a poor judge of your own performance in the moment.
- **Reset between rounds** (see below) and treat the next round as a fresh start.

### When the interviewer seems disengaged

They're probably typing notes, which is good: notes are evidence. Keep going and check in: "Would you like me to go deeper here or move on?"

### Recovery phrases

| Situation | Say |
|---|---|
| You need thinking time | "Give me 20 seconds to think this through." |
| You realise you made a mistake | "Actually, I want to correct something I said: …" |
| You misunderstood the question | "Let me make sure I'm answering what you asked. Did you mean X or Y?" |
| You're running out of time | "In the time left, I'll prioritise X and describe how I'd handle Y." |
| You got a hint | "That's a helpful pointer. So if I use a map here, then…" |
| You lost your train of thought | "Let me step back and summarise where I am." |

## Questions to ask interviewers

Always have questions. Asking good ones signals genuine interest and seniority, and it's your chance to evaluate them. Ask **2–3 per interviewer**, tailored to their role.

### Engineers

- "What does a typical week look like for you? How much is building versus meetings or support?"
- "How does a change get from a pull request to production, and how long does that take?"
- "What gives the team confidence to ship frontend changes: tests, previews, feature flags?"
- "If you could change one thing about the frontend architecture, what would it be?"
- "How are technical decisions made: RFCs, architecture reviews, informal discussion?"
- "Is there a design system? Who owns it, and how do teams contribute?"
- "How is the team using AI coding tools, and what guardrails are in place?"

### Engineering manager

- "What would success look like for this role after 90 days and after a year?"
- "What's the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?"
- "How do you balance product work with technical debt and quality?"
- "How do you give feedback, and how does someone grow to the next level here?"
- "What did the person who most excelled in this kind of role do differently?"
- "How is the team composed, and where would I fit in?"

### Director, skip-level or VP

- "What are the organisation's top priorities for the next year, and how does this team contribute?"
- "What's the biggest risk to achieving those goals?"
- "How do engineering, product and design work together here?"
- "How do you measure whether an engineering team is healthy?"
- "Where do you see the web platform or frontend going strategically for the company?"

### Recruiter

- "What are the next steps and the timeline for a decision?"
- "Which level is this role scoped at, and what's the compensation range?"
- "Is there anything I should prepare for the next rounds?"

> [!MISTAKE]
> Asking things answered on the careers page, asking engineers about compensation, or asking "Did I pass?". Also avoid negative framing ("How bad is on-call?"); ask "What's on-call like, and how is it supported?" instead.

## Between rounds

A 2–5-minute reset routine:

1. Stand up, stretch, drink water.
2. Jot **three bullets** about the round you just finished (the questions, anything you'd follow up on). Then let it go: no replaying.
3. Read the opening script for the next round and glance at your questions for that interviewer.
4. One slow breath before you join.

## Thank-you note

Within **24 hours**, usually to the recruiter (and to interviewers if you have their addresses and it fits the company's culture). Keep it to 3–5 sentences with one specific detail. It won't win an offer on its own, but it's professional and occasionally lets you fix a mistake.

```text
Subject: Thank you, [Role] interviews

Hi [Name],

Thank you for today's conversations. I especially enjoyed discussing [specific topic,
e.g. how the team is migrating the dashboard to server components] with [interviewer].
It reinforced my interest in [team/product], particularly [reason that ties to your experience].

One follow-up from the coding round: I realised afterwards that my debounce should also
clear the pending timer in cancel(); the fix is a single clearTimeout call.
(Optional: one line only, and only if it's genuinely useful.)

Looking forward to hearing about next steps.
[Your name]
```

## Debrief afterwards

Do it **within an hour**, while it's fresh. Memory of the questions fades fast, and those questions are the best study material you'll ever get.

| Round | Questions asked (verbatim) | What went well | What went badly | Self-score vs rubric (1–4) | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| e.g. Coding | Implement `retry` with backoff; follow-up on cancellation | Clarified well; clean types | Forgot to test the rejection path | 3 | Re-do the async exercises; add a flashcard on `AbortSignal` |
| … | | | | | |

1. **Brain-dump** every question and follow-up.
2. **Score yourself** against the rubric tables in the playbook (coding, UI build, system design, behavioural).
3. Pick the **top 1–2 gaps** and turn each into a concrete action: re-do an exercise, run a targeted mock, add a story to the bank.
4. Note what **worked**, so you repeat it.
5. Update your story bank with any new follow-up questions you were asked.

**If you get a rejection:** ask the recruiter for feedback ("Is there anything you can share about where I could improve?"). Many companies share little; take what you get. Interview outcomes are noisy: one rejection is a data point, not a verdict. Re-application cool-down periods are common, so ask about the timing if you'd like to try again.

## Key takeaways

- The night before: logistics, setup test, two stories and your opener out loud, questions written, then rest.
- The morning: a 20-minute warm-up of things you already know, spoken out loud. Nothing new.
- Nerves are energy: breathe, reframe, and remember the interviewer wants you to succeed.
- When you blank, pause on purpose, restate, and fall back on the structure.
- Ask 2–3 tailored questions per interviewer; send a short thank-you; debrief within an hour and turn gaps into actions.
